**Memory CD8<sup>+</sup> T cells and Long-Term Hematopoietic Stem Cells Share a Transcriptional Profile of Self-Renewal**

<span class="underline">Abstract</span>

The only cells in the adult hematopoietic system that undergo self-renewal are long-term hematopoietic stem cells and memory T and B cells. We set out to determine whether this similar characteristic arises from a common molecular program. The transcriptional profiles of memory, naïve and effector CD8<sup>+</sup> T cells were compared with long-term hematopoietic stem cells, short-term hematopoietic stem cells, and lineage committed progenitors. Transcripts enriched in memory CD8<sup>+</sup> T cells relative to naïve and effector T cells were also selectively enriched in long-term hematopoietic stem cells relative to their short-term and lineage committed counterparts. Thus, the capacity for self-renewal shared by memory T cells and long-term hematopoietic stem cells correlates with a common molecular program. Several of these shared transcripts have been shown to be involved in self-renewal*.* We propose that these common transcripts provide a unique insight into the molecular pathways involved in homeostatic self-renewal within the adult hematopoietic system. *Study of these molecular pathways will be essential in understanding the molecular basis for the maintenance of long-term immunological memory as well as steady state hematopoiesis.*

<span class="underline">Introduction</span>

One hallmark of the adaptive immune response is it’s the ability to respond more effectively upon re-encountering a pathogen—thus “remembering” a prior pathogenic exposure. The ‘memory’ response that results upon re-exposure to a pathogen is larger and more rapid than the initial ‘naïve’ response, providing a more robust control of the infection. Memory responses to most pathogens require the development of memory T and B cells, which along with long-lived antibody secreting B cells called plasma cells provide the cellular basis for successful vaccination.

Recent developments in the enumeration and characterization of antigen-specific CD8<sup>+</sup> T cell populations have allowed for a more detailed picture of the cellular dynamics underlying the formation and maintenace of T cell memory (reviewed in \[1-4\]). 

Prior to initial infection, antigen-inexperienced, “naïve” T cells that may recognize a given infectious agent exist at very low frequencies and rarely if ever divide \[6, 7\].. Upon pathogen exposure, those rare naïve T cells capable of recognizing the pathogen via their TCR undergo a process of rapid clonal expansion as well as differentiation. These stimulated T cells divide as rapidly as every 6-8 hours and acquire the necessary functional activity to eliminate the pathogen; these cells are termed “effector” T cells (make this all CD8<sup>+</sup> specific) \[8, 9\]. Effector T cells demonstrate a more rapid response to antigen stimulation and the ability to traffic throughout the body. CD8<sup>+</sup> effector T cells undergo a dramatic contraction in numbers following antigen clearance (with 90-95% of the antigen specific CD8<sup>+</sup> T cells undergoing apoptosis within two weeks of initial infection \[10\].

For CD8<sup>+</sup> T cells, a subset of the antigen-specific persists long after initial antigen exposure and are known as memory cells. The lineage relationship between effector T cells and memory T cells has been the subject of several recent papers. Initial reports indicated that naive progeny destined to become memory T cells underwent a stage in which they adopted effector-like phenotype and gene expression patterns, suggesting that memory T cells were derived stochastically from a homogeneous effector T cell pool \[11\]. However, more recent work has demonstrated that the precursors destined to become memory cells can be identified among the effector T cell population by the surface expression of IL-7R and CD8 alpha/alpha homodimer \[12-14\]. IL-7R and CD8 alpha/alpha expression distinguishes memory-destined effector T cells from the majority of effector T cells that are fated to die in the contraction phase of the immune response. Thus it appears that all effector T cells are not created equally, with only a subset expressing the necessary transcripts that allow for the progression to long-lived memory T cells.

Followinginfection, memory CD8<sup>+</sup> T cells are maintained at relatively constant cell numbers often for the lifetime of an individual \[9, 10\]. Once formed, memory CD8<sup>+</sup> T cells undergo a process of homeostatic self-renewal or basal homeostatic proliferation which is dependent on IL-15 . Thus, memory T cells numbers are maintained through a process involving a homeostatic balancing of low level turn-over and survival with death. \[1, 6\]. (you said this already) It is this persistentence of an increased precursor frequency long-lived memory cells, combined with their rapid ability to differentiate into secondary effectors, that provides the basis for the robust recall response to re-infection in successful vaccination.

We are particularly interested in understanding the molecular events that allow memory T cells to persist and thus provide long lasting antigen-specific protection over the lifetime of an individual. The ability of memory T cells to proliferate in response to homeostatic signals without further differentiation is strikingly reminiscent of the self-renewal observed in hematopoietic stem cell populations \[15\]. The capacity for self-renewal observed by both memory CD8<sup>+</sup> T cells and long-term hematopoietic stem cells appears to support the maintainance of relatively constant numbers throughout the life of the animal. Furthermore, memory T cells and Lt-HSC also maintain the ability to differentiate into progeny which are incapable of self renewal. While these phenotypic similarities have been pointed out previously \[2, 16, 17\], no direct evidence that either supports or contradicts these general observations has been reported. We, therefore, set out to directly test the hypothesis that memory T cells and long-term hematopoietic stem cells share a portion of their respective transcriptional profiles. We hypothesized that those transcripts expressed in memory T cells would be enriched in long-term hematopoietic stem cells and would likely be involved in the molecular pathways of self-renewal.

<span class="underline">Results</span>

In order to compare the overall transcriptional profiles of memory CD8<sup>+</sup> T cell populations with hematopoietic stem cell populations, we employed Affymetrix Genechip analysis. As a source of monoclonal populations of naïve, effector, and memory CD8<sup>+</sup> T cells, we used the OTI TCR-transgenic adoptive transfer and viral infection model described previously (manuscript in press). Purified naïve, effector, and memory populations expressed the expected cell surface phenotypic markers and demonstrated functional activities indicative of naïve, effector and memory CD8<sup>+</sup> T cells (data not shown). For stem cell populations, we chose the phenotypically well-characterized and functionally defined mature hematopoietic stem cell data published by Ivanova et.al. \[18\].

Having established functionally defined cellular populations, we grouped together the raw data (.cel files) and collectively pre-processed them with the “rma” method in the “affylmGUI” statistical analysis package developed by the collaborative, open source project [www.bioconductor.org](http://www.bioconductor.org/) \[19-22\]. The rma method uses an improved algorithm for probe level background correction, normalization, and summary that dramatically reduces observed statistical noise both in published control data sets \[19-21\] as well as among replicates within our own data (data not shown). Differential expression of individual genes was calculated using the LIMMA (LInear Models for MicroArray data) within the affylmGUI package \[23\]. LIMMA uses empirical Bayesian methods to provide stable results even when number of replicates between arrays is relatively small, as is the case with these data sets.

We confirmed that our data set accurately depicts the expression profiles of naïve, effector, and memory cells by showing that known transcripts behave as expected (manuscript in preparation). Furthermore, our expression profiles are remarkably similar to those generated independently using a different TCR transgenic and viral infection model by Kaech et.al. \[11\], providing an important external validation of our techniques and cell purity. Finally, we demonstrated that memory CD8<sup>+</sup> T cells generated in our model are self-renewing by following memory cell numbers from a cohort of mice over the course of several months. OTI memory CD8<sup>+</sup> T cells maintained constant cell numbers, cell surface phenotypes, and cytokine secretion profiles (data not shown). Our findings are consistent with the published reports demonstrating that CD8<sup>+</sup> memory T cells homeostatically self- renew over the life of an individual \[9, 10\].

In order to test whether memory CD8<sup>+</sup> T cells and Lt-HSC share a transcriptional profile, we first compared memory versus naïve T cells and the long-term versus short-term hematopoietic stem cells. These pairs were chosen because they represented the closest relatives to each other when all genes were compared either by MVA plots or cluster analysis (data not shown). Furthermore, since both Lt-HSC and St-HSC populations are pluripotent, the only known functional difference between them is the inability of St-HSC to self-renew \[15\]. Finally, memory T cells divide more frequently than naïve cells while St-HSC divide more frequently than Lt-HSC, thus any similarities observed between memory T cells and Lt-HSC not likely to be the result a proliferative molecular signature. These comparisons therefore represented a focused look at self-renewing potential.

Interestingly, those genes that were differentially enriched in memory T cells were dramatically skewed toward Lt-HSC, with 73% of the transcripts enriched relative to St-HSC (**Figure 1A**). In addition to the number of transcripts, there was a clear skewing towards more significant P values among the Lt-HSC enriched genes. The reverse comparison was similarly skewed. Of the transcripts differentially enriched in long- versus short-term hematopoietic stem cells, 62% were also enriched in memory T cells relative to naïve T cells and showed a trend towards more significant P values (**Figure 1B**). This demonstrates a significant overlap between the expression profiles of self-renewing memory cells and self-renewing long-term hematopoietic stem cells, at least when they are compared to their closest non-renewing relatives: naïve T cells and St-HSC.

We next took a broader look at how the memory-enriched genes in Figure 1 were expressed across all mature hematopoietic precursor populations. Consistent with the idea that memory-enhanced genes relative to naive are shared with Lt-HSC and not other hematopoietic precursor populations, memory enriched genes demonstrated a positive skewing in the Lt-HSC versus LCP (68%), with less visual skewing but similar percentages of St-HSC versus LCP (67%) comparisons (**Figure 2A**). This demonstrates that memory genes enriched relative to naïve cells are selectively enriched in long-term hematopoietic stem cells.

Despite the clear correlation of memory-enriched genes with Lt-HSC in all pair-wise comparisons, the converse did not hold to be true. Specifically, Lt-HSC enriched genes were not strongly correlated with memory T cells when compared to effector T cells (51%) (**Figure 2B**). Indeed, the enrichment seen in the memory versus naïve cells was comparable to that observed for effector relative to naïve (56%). It appears that while the set of transcripts most up regulated in memory T cells are generally up in Lt-HSC, the transcripts most up regulated in Lt-HSC are actually negatively correlated with naïve expression.

As outlined in the introduction, recent reports have demonstrated that effector CD8<sup>+</sup> T cells are heterogeneous \[12-14\]. A subset of effector T cells at the peak of the initial burst already express a few memory cell surface markers (IL-7R and CD8 alpha/alpha), and these effectors appear to go one to become self-renewing memory cells. We therefore hypothesized that the positive correlation observed between Lt-HSC and effectors was a result of correlation with the subset of IL-7R<sup>+</sup> memory-destined effectors that might themselves express a more memory-like phenotype. We sorted day 8 effector T cells into IL-7R<sup>+</sup> and IL-7R<sup>-</sup> populations and subjected them to Affymetrix Genechip analysis. To our surprise, there was no selective enrichment of the Lt-HSC transcripts in IL-7R<sup>+</sup> effector cells relative to their IL-7R<sup>-</sup> counterparts (**Figure 3A**). Furthermore, Lt-HSC specific genes where selectively enriched in IL-7R<sup>-</sup> effectors relative to naïve cells (**Figure 3B**). This confirmed our previous observation that much of what is up-regulated in Lt-HSC vs. St-HSC actually represents genes that are selectively down-regulated in naïve cells relative to both memory and all effector populations.

Given the above results, we wanted to ensure that the shared transcripts between memory and Lt-HSC observed in Figure 1A were not solely the result of the comparison to naive and St-HSC cells, i.e. a denominator effect. We therefore looked at the relative expression of the subset of genes that met our definition of differential expression in memory T cells in both the naïve as well as the effector comparisons. Of the 46 transcripts that met these criteria, there was again a significant skewing of both numbers and P values towards Lt-HSC compared with St-HSC (65%) (**Figure 4**). Thus a significant fraction of memory specific transcripts were selectively enriched in Lt-HSC. This demonstrates that a common molecular signature is shared between memory CD8<sup>+</sup> T cells and long-term hematopoietic stem cells.

In order to gain further insight into these shared transcripts, we made a list of all transcripts which were up-regulated in memory cells compared to naïve and effector cells as well as up-regulated in Lt-HSC relative to St-HSC (**Table I**). Our inclusion criteria of fold change of 1.4 was based on within-replicate analyses providing empirical estimation of experimental noise (data not shown). By quantitative PCR, we have found that this cutoff correctly predicts differential expression 93% of the time, with the GeneChip data often dramatically underestimating true fold changes (manuscript in preparation). Such underestimation is consistent both with general array data and further reflects the fact that the ‘rma’ algorithm consistently underestimates true fold changes; with the decrease in the accuracy of the algorithm serving as a tradeoff for a dramatic increase in its precision \[19-21\].

We were pleased to discover that Mel18 was among those shared transcripts between memory and Lt-HSC. Mel18 is a polycomb family member which, like its closest homolog BMI1, has been shown to be involved in the self-renewal of hematopoietic stem cells \[24\] and is involved in inhibition of the of Ink4a/ARF complex \[25\]. Interestingly, Mel18 knockout mice also demonstrate a T cell differentiation defect during thymic and CD4<sup>+</sup> differentiation, but its role in memory CD8<sup>+</sup> T cells has yet to be addressed \[26, 27\]. There were several other shared transcripts with known functionsconsistent with their potentially playing a role in stem cell self-renewal. Both TnfR2 and Traf1 are known to associate within the cell, and signaling via this pathway results in inhibition of apoptosis (reviewed in \[28, 29\]. Finally, the stromal-derived cytokine EDR has been shown to play a role in the survival of several different cell types \[30\]. Taken together, the presence of these transcripts previously been shown to play a role in self-renewal and/or survival supports the general hypothesis that the shared transcripts in both memory CD8<sup>+</sup> T cells and Lt-HSC represent a self-renewal molecular signature.

We were interested in determining whether the transcripts shared between memory and Lt-HSC were also enriched in other stem cell comparisons. In order to address this issue, we pooled together our T cell data with the complete published data sets from Ivanova et.al., including their adult and fetal hematopoietic stem cells as well as embryonic and neural stem cells \[18\]; the data sets from Ramalho-Santos et. al. which included adult hematopoietic stem cells as well as neural and embryonic stem cells \[31\]; and the data sets from Akashi et. al. which included adult LtHSC and StHSC populations \[32\]. These data sets were normalized together and analyzed as above using affylmGUI. Intriguingly, the vast majority of those genes whose expression we had previously identified as enriched in memory T cells and LtHSC were also enriched in the adult hematopoietic stem cell populations from Ramalho-Santos et.al. and Akashi et.al. (**Table II**). Furthermore, most of the shared transcripts were also enriched in fetal liver hematopoietic precursor populations. This provided important external confirmation of our findings, suggesting that this shared signature holds true for all hematopoietic self-renewing populations tested. However, there was not a consistent enrichment in either NSC or ESC populations (data not shown). Therefore this signature does not seem to be conserved with stem cell populations outside of the hematopoietic system.

# Discussion

Several authors have posited that memory T cells are similar to stem cells in their ability to both self-renew and maintain the ability for further differentiation \[2, 16, 17\]. However, there has been no previous evidence to support that these shared phenotypes result from a shared molecular program. Given that Long-term hematopoietic stem cells and memory T and B cells are the only cells of the adult hematopoietic system which undergo homeostatic self-renewal, we tested whether Lt-HSC and memory CD8+ T cells shared a common molecular profile.

We have shown that Lt-HSC and memory CD8+ T cells share a significant portion of their transcriptional profiles when compared to their closest non-renewing counterparts: St-HSC and naïve CD8<sup>+</sup> T cells. Further analysis demonstrated that many of these shared transcripts were also selectively enriched in effector CD8<sup>+</sup> T cells relative to naïve cells, suggesting that a subset of Lt-HSC enriched transcripts are selectively down-regulated in naïve CD8<sup>+</sup> T cells relative to their memory and effector counterparts. This was surprising given the relative quiescence of both naïve \[33\] and Lt-HSC populations \[32\]. We suggest this correlation reflects a fundamental difference in the overall transcriptional accessibility of the two populations, with naïve cells in a closed chromatin state relative to the known open state of Lt-HSC (reviewed in \[34\]).

Given our interest in directly comparing Lt-HSC with memory T cells, we further differentiated memory specific transcripts based on their enrichment relative to both naïve and effector T cells. Once again, memory specific transcripts were enriched in Lt-HSC. Further analysis of the specific shared transcripts demonstrated that several are known to play a role in self-renewal and/or cell survival. This provides the first evidence that Lt-HSC and memory T cells share a common molecular program. Furthermore, we suggest that these shared transcripts represent a self-renewal signature for the hematopoietic system.

There has been a great deal of debate within the literature concerning the validity and reproducibility of defining a general molecular signature of stem cells \[18, 31, 35, 36\]. We took several steps to minimize potential differences due to analytical methods. We chose to analyze only those data files where we were able to obtain the original Affymetrix cell intensity file (.cel file). All cel files were normalized and summarized at the probe level together, and were statistically analyzed in exactly the same manner. In contrast to the previous attempts to broadly define a stemness profile, we chose to focus on the more limited phenotype of self-renewal within the hematopoietic system. We feel that by using memory T cells, which arguably share only this phenotypic characteristic with other stem cell populations, we might gain a unique and focused insight into self-renewal. Indeed, our analysis demonstrates that those transcripts shared between memory CD8<sup>+</sup> T cells and Lt-HSC are generally enriched in other adult, and to a lesser degree fetal, hematopoietic stem cell populations. However, they are not enriched in NSC and ESC populations, consistent with the idea that the specific transcripts involved in self-renewal are only conserved within a particular differentiation system \[35\].

We feel that the shared transcripts identified in the study are excellent candidates for playing a direct functional role in stem cell and memory T cell self-renewal. Mel18 has been shown to affect Lt-HSC self-renewal, but its role in memory has not been explored. While TnfR2 and Traf1 are known to be involved in anti-apoptotic signaling; their role in the self-renewal of either T cells or HSC has not been directly addressed. Similarly, the stromal derived cytokine EDR has been shown to support the survival of multiple hematopoietic cell types *in vitro* but its role *in vivo* has not been addressed. In addition, the presence of several specific members of the RAS/MAP-Kinase pathway suggests that selective activation of individual members of this diverse signaling pathway are invoved in self-renewal.

While IL-7R has been shown to be essential for the long-term survival of memory CD8<sup>+</sup> T cells \[1\], its presence in the Lt-HSC transcriptional profile points to one of the limitations of the current study. Our data relies solely on transcriptional information, and the IL-7R protein is not expressed on the surface of Lt-HSC \[32\]. Obviously, relative protein expression and protein interactions/ modifications would be a more informative comparison. Given the small numbers of cells available from the populations studied, such comparisons will require advancement in the sensitivity and dynamic range of current mass-spectrometric proteomic technologies. For now, transcriptional profiling remains one of the more tractable quantitative methods for global comparative analysis between rare cellular populations.

We have demonstrated that most of the transcripts uniquely enriched in memory CD8<sup>+</sup> T cells are also enriched in long-term hematopoietic stems cells, implying they represent a signature for the molecular process of self-renewal. We believe the subset of memory enriched genes that are also enriched in long-term hematopoietic stem cells are excellent candidates for a common molecular signature of self-renewal within the hematopoietic system. Further analysis of the functional requirement for these individual transcripts for the self-renewal of both memory T cells and Lt-HSC should prove enlightening.
